## Today's Highlights

A new spectral-scaling analysis of 671 U.S. watersheds reveals that streamflow persistence is shaped by both climate forcing and catchment memory, identifying three distinct regimes — rapid-response, forcing-persistent, and hydrologically buffered — with important implications for how land-surface and routing models should represent subsurface storage. On the coastal side, a global synthesis of 332 sediment cores revises downward the carbon burial capacity of seagrass meadows, showing they store 6–15 Tg C yr⁻¹ globally — still a meaningful fraction of total oceanic organic carbon burial despite covering less than 0.1% of the seafloor.

## Top-Tier Journal Papers

### Temporal Scaling of Hydrological Signals Reveals Forcing Persistence and Watershed Filtering Across the Contiguous U.S.

**Authors**: Yunqiu Zhou, Yi Zheng, Yingjie Yang, Xiuyu Liang

**Journal**: *Geophysical Research Letters* · **DOI**: [10.1029/2026gl124564](https://doi.org/10.1029/2026gl124564)

**Matched topics**: streamflow
{: .label .label-green }

> Streamflow temporal persistence reflects both hydrological inputs and watershed filtering, but their relative contributions remain difficult to distinguish. We analyzed spectral scaling in precipitation, evapotranspiration, effective precipitation, streamflow, and the effective precipitation‐to‐streamflow transfer function across 671 contiguous U.S. watersheds. Streamflow and transfer‐function scaling exponents vary more widely across watersheds than those of the hydrological inputs. Combining effective‐precipitation and transfer‐function exponents identifies three regimes: rapid‐response watersheds with weak input persistence and filtering, widespread across the central and eastern U.S.; forcing‐persistent watersheds with persistent inputs but limited filtering, mainly in the West; and hydrologically buffered watersheds with strong filtering, concentrated in the western mountains, northern regions and Southeast. Western mountain buffered watersheds have greater snow influence and baseflow contributions, whereas southeastern buffered watersheds have little snow but more permeable soils and subsurface materials. The two exponents distinguish input persistence from watershed filtering in shaping streamflow persistence.

### Seagrass sediment organic carbon burial rates are globally significant

**Authors**: Ariane Arias-Ortiz, Anna Lafratta, Phil Colarusso, James W. Fourqurean, Chuancheng Fu, Hilary Kennedy et al.

**Journal**: *Nature Communications* · **DOI**: [10.1038/s41467-026-79168-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-79168-3)

**Matched topics**: coastal
{: .label .label-green }

![Figure](https://media.springernature.com/m685/springer-static/image/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41467-026-79168-3/MediaObjects/41467_2026_79168_Fig1_HTML.png)

> Crucial for coastal carbon sequestration, global seagrass organic carbon (OC) burial rates remain uncertain as early estimates were derived from mixed methodologies across geographically and taxonomically biased datasets. Here, we present a data-driven reassessment from a synthesis of 332 sediment cores spanning the last century. Results show that seagrasses bury OC at 25 ± 3 g OC m−2 yr−1 (geometric mean), with an area-weighted global average of 33 ± 9 g OC m−2 yr−1. Rates reflect a balance between sediment OC content and sediment accumulation, which share a negative exponential relationship driven by mineral dilution. Based on a revised mapped seagrass extent (247,700–366,400 km2), global burial scales to 6–15 Tg C yr−1. Although one-fourth of earlier benchmarks, seagrasses account for 3–4% to 3–6% of total oceanic OC burial despite covering <0.1% of the seafloor. Combined with mangroves and tidal marshes, vegetated coastal ecosystems contribute 8–13% of oceanic OC burial.
