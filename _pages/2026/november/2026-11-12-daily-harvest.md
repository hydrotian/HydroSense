## Today's Highlights

A landmark Science study by Bo Su et al. formally establishes that Earth's cryosphere has breached its planetary safe operating space, with accelerating and partly irreversible loss now locking in long-term risks for water systems and human societies. Complementing this, a machine-learning analysis of 2.9 million river segments shows that climate aridity—not only anthropogenic pollution—systematically amplifies riverine nitrate concentrations globally, exposing 5.6 billion people to ecological threshold exceedances; as aridification advances, these risks could grow even without added human inputs. A Science perspective also highlights the growing inadequacy of traditional mountain monitoring systems as cryospheric cascades reshape hazard patterns in high-elevation watersheds.

## Top-Tier Journal Papers

### Tracking danger on the peaks

**Authors**: Miriam Jackson, Amrit Thapa

**Journal**: *Science* · **DOI**: [10.1126/science.aen7366](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aen7366)

**Matched topics**: river, flood
{: .label .label-green }

> The Earth is warming, but not all regions are warming equally. In high mountain areas, temperatures are generally increasing by 1.5 to 2 times the global average, sometimes with devastating consequences. A small or gradual change in the mountain cryosphere-perhaps progressive thawing of frozen ground or a flood from a small glacial lake-can suddenly turn into a cascade of catastrophes with enormous ramifications. Monitoring systems for the cryosphere in high mountain areas have traditionally been designed to look at individual components and changes. What are the temperature and depth of the permafrost, or how much area is covered by snow, and what is the related avalanche danger? Many of the monitoring and warning systems were designed for historical patterns of snowfall or rainfall, and a river gauge may no longer be adequate if there is greater flow in the rainy season, or increased sediment that could damage the gauge.

### The cryosphere beyond a planetary safe operating space

**Authors**: Bo Su, Johan Rockström, Deliang Chen, Ingo Fetzer, Haipeng Feng, Gustaf Hugelius et al.

**Journal**: *Science* · **DOI**: [10.1126/science.aef5636](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aef5636)

**Matched topics**: Holocene
{: .label .label-green }

> The cryosphere plays a vital role in regulating planetary dynamics and habitability, yet it remains unclear whether its essential functions remain within a safe operating space (SOS) amid Anthropocene change. Here, we propose a cryosphere SOS and systematically evaluate its contributions to Earth system resilience using the planetary boundaries framework. Multiple lines of evidence indicate an accelerating and partly irreversible loss of cryospheric integrity, driven by amplifying feedbacks. Key cryospheric control variables either have left or are committed to departing from Holocene-like conditions. We conclude that the cryosphere has breached its SOS and is on a trajectory that locks in long-term risks for human societies and ecosystems. Safeguarding planetary resilience therefore requires explicit consideration of cryosphere change and its interactions across the Earth system.

### Climate aridity amplifies global riverine nitrate beyond human inputs

**Authors**: Kayalvizhi Sadayappan, Li Li

**Journal**: *Science* · **DOI**: [10.1126/science.aeg2366](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aeg2366)

**Matched topics**: river
{: .label .label-green }

> Nitrate pollution is widely perceived as a signature of human inputs. Here we show, with machine-learning reconstructed global maps, that climate aridity is also a dominant driver, systematically elevating nitrate concentrations across land-use gradients and creating unexpected hotspots beyond major agricultural and urban centers. Across ~2.9 million river segments worldwide, nitrate concentrations exceed ecological and health thresholds across ~33 and 2% of global land area, affecting ~5.6 billion (73%) and 0.5 billion (6%) people, respectively. Ecological threshold exceedance occurs mostly in headwaters, and about half of the health threshold exceedance occurs in middle-income countries and sparsely populated landscapes. These findings cast nitrate pollution as an aridity-amplified risk, suggesting that aridification may increase nitrate exposure even without additional human inputs. They call for regulatory standards that reflect risks across broad concentration ranges.

### Cross-discipline sparks
