## Today's Highlights

Fifteen years of atmospheric CO₂ measurements across Amazonia reveal that the basin has become a net carbon source of 0.32 ± 0.08 PgC/yr, with fires now overwhelming the forest's dwindling uptake capacity. Human activities alone — deforestation and land degradation — account for 0.43 PgC/yr in years of low climatic stress, but drought-dominated years such as 2010, 2015–2016, and 2023–2024 spike emissions to 0.49 PgC/yr, demonstrating an alarming synergy between land-use change and climate extremes. For large-scale hydrology, the findings underscore how Amazonian drought-driven feedbacks — reduced precipitation recycling, heightened flammability, lengthening dry seasons — could amplify regional water cycle disruption far beyond the immediate fire footprint.

## Top-Tier Journal Papers

### Amazon carbon emissions driven by human actions amplified by droughts

**Authors**: Luciana V. Gatti, Camilla L. Cunha, Juan C. G. Blas, A. Scott Denning, Mariane S. Reis, Luciano Marani et al.

**Journal**: *Nature* · **DOI**: [10.1038/s41586-026-11168-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11168-1)

**Matched topics**: river, drought, climate change
{: .label .label-green }

![Figure](https://media.springernature.com/m685/springer-static/image/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41586-026-11168-1/MediaObjects/41586_2026_11168_Fig1_HTML.png)

> The Amazon carbon sink is weakening as deforestation, rising tree mortality and climate change disrupt the climatic conditions that sustain a humid tropical forest. Forest loss reduces precipitation, increases temperatures and lengthens the dry season, driving higher carbon emissions. Climate stress is stronger in more deforested regions, rendering forests progressively more flammable. Large-scale climate events, such as El Niño, further amplify regional climate impacts. Here we present an analysis of 15 years of Amazonian carbon fluxes derived from atmospheric CO2 measurements. We determine a carbon balance of 0.32 ± 0.08 PgC yr−1, with fires acting as the dominant source (0.40 ± 0.07 PgC yr−1) and forest uptake becoming increasingly unable to offset emissions. Furthermore, we investigate key drivers of the carbon emissions of Amazonia and identify three distinct year groups. Group 1 (2011–2014, 2017 and 2018) represents the role of the Amazon in the global carbon balance (0.11 ± 0.08 PgC yr−1) during wetter years with low human activity, such as deforestation, biomass burning and degradation. Group 2 (2019–2022) represents Amazon carbon emissions (0.43 ± 0.13 PgC yr−1) from human activities (mainly deforestation and degradation) during years with low climatic stress. Group 3 (2010, 2015, 2016, 2023 and 2024) has the highest carbon emissions (0.49 ± 0.07 PgC yr−1) and comprises warm and dry years (high climate stress), such as El Niño and the warming of the Tropical North Atlantic Ocean, associated with biomass burning.
