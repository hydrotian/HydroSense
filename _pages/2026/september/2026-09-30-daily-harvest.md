## Top-Tier Journal Papers

### Committed Antarctic Ice Sheet mass loss by the end of the twenty-first century

**Authors**: Yucheng Lin, Xuebin Zhang, Nicholas R. Golledge, Robert E. Kopp, John A. Church, Yi Jin et al.

**Journal**: *Nature Geoscience* · **DOI**: [10.1038/s41561-026-02102-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41561-026-02102-1)

**Matched topics**: coastal
{: .label .label-green }

![Figure](https://media.springernature.com/m685/springer-static/image/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41561-026-02102-1/MediaObjects/41561_2026_2102_Fig1_HTML.png)

> The Antarctic Ice Sheet is the largest source of uncertainty in sea-level rise projections, with uncertainties propagating through emissions scenarios, atmosphere–ocean general circulation models, ice-sheet dynamics and sea-level physics. In ice-sheet model intercomparison exercises, these uncertainties—typically attributed to intermodel differences—stem from modelling choices that may bias ensembles towards commonly adopted approaches regardless of observational consistency. Here we quantify how each individual physical assumption cascades into projection uncertainty using a machine-learning emulation framework, which also enables Bayesian calibration against satellite observations to reduce projection bias. Our results suggest it is very likely (≥0.92 probability) that the Antarctic Ice Sheet is committed to twenty-first-century mass loss, even under aggressive emissions-reduction scenarios. Higher emissions drive greater Antarctic mass loss by 2100 (≥0.89 probability), directly elevating near-term coastal risks. Under very high-emissions scenarios, we identify cascading mechanisms that could produce up to 25.4 cm of sea-level rise by 2100 (95th percentile; median = 15.7 cm) while remaining consistent with satellite observations. Effective management of these risks to densely populated coastal communities requires rapid emissions reductions and improved constraints on climate model selection, sliding laws and ice-shelf melt parameterizations.

**Journal**: *Nature Water* · **DOI**: [10.1038/s44221-026-00716-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44221-026-00716-8)

**Matched topics**: seasonal
{: .label .label-green }

![Figure](https://media.springernature.com/m685/springer-static/image/art%3A10.1038%2Fs44221-026-00716-8/MediaObjects/44221_2026_716_Fig1_HTML.png)

> A whole-watershed experiment revealed rapid streamwater alkalinity increases within weeks of basalt application, with strong seasonal cycling and the export of ~10% of theoretical carbon removal as alkalinity over 2 years, underscoring that temperature and hydrologic variability critically govern enhanced weathering effectiveness and monitoring.

### How AI is changing research

- **[Retrofitting language models to operate over bytes](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11111-4)** (Nature, 2026-10-08) — A new architecture retrofits LLMs to operate over raw byte sequences instead of tokenized text, eliminating the tokenization bottleneck. ### Cross-discipline sparks

- **[Australia's current wildfire crisis linked to colonial land-use change](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11097-z)** (Nature, 2026-10-08) — Researchers tied modern wildfire intensification in Australia directly to post-1788 colonial land-use disruption using historical fire records and present-day remote sensing. The cross-discipline angle for earth scientists: the same approach—integrating colonial-era archival records, paleoecological proxies, and geospatial time series—is now tractable for a small team using AI to mine historical documents and link them to land-surface model inputs, enabling long-term baseline shifts in land cover and disturbance regimes to be embedded in ESM simulations.
